Worst-Case Required Torque

A grid search sweeps every combination of shoulder/elbow/wrist angle inside your configured joint-limit ranges and keeps the maximum gravity torque found for each joint independently — this is the true worst case your hardware can actually reach, not just an assumed "flat horizontal" pose. Dynamic (inertial) torque adds the point-mass and each link's own rotational inertia (mL²/12, parallel-axis) at your target angular acceleration. Friction margin and safety factor are applied last.

How to read the motor cards below

Enter a candidate motor's stall torque (the datasheet headline number), your gear ratio and gearbox efficiency. The tool derates stall→continuous, multiplies by gear ratio and efficiency, and compares against the required torque above. If you know your motor's actual rated continuous torque, put that in the stall field instead and set derating to 100% in Setup. Optional rotor inertia lets you account for the extra torque needed just to spin up the motor's own rotor through the gearbox.

What should the links themselves weigh?

Each link is modelled as a cantilever tube, fixed at the proximal joint, loaded at the tip by everything it carries downstream (links, payload, and any actuator mass physically riding on that link at its far end) in the worst-case horizontal orientation. The tool solves for the minimum tube outer diameter — at your chosen wall thickness and material — that keeps bending stress under an allowable stress (yield ÷ material safety factor), checks tip deflection too, and reports the governing mass. This is simple beam theory, not FEA — treat it as a floor, not a final spec.

Method

This arm's real geometry (the 180° elbow twist, offset base-to-shoulder rise) has no clean closed-form IK the way a pure planar 3-link arm does, so this solves numerically: damped least-squares on the position Jacobian (θ ← θ + Jᵀ(JJᵀ+λ²I)⁻¹·error), iterated from your current Live Pose tab angles as the starting seed. Different seeds can converge to different elbow configurations — nudge the Live Pose sliders and re-solve if you want a different one.
θ5 (wrist roll) never appears in this solve — rolling about the gripper's own axis can't move the tip position (the TCP sits on that axis by construction), so it's a free DOF for position-only targets and is left wherever your Live Pose slider currently has it.

Two independent computations of the same tip position at your current Live Pose: the live 4×4 matrix chain ⁰T₆ = A₁A₂A₃A₄A₅A₆, and a separately-derived symbolic closed-form expression (computer-algebra derived once, not hand-simplified). Gripper Force: Required vs. Achievable

Holding an object without it slipping needs enough clamp force to generate friction ≥ its weight: F_required = (payload weight × SF) / (contact points × friction coefficient). What the actuator can actually produce is F_achievable = (torque × mechanical advantage) / lever arm. Compare the two — a gripper can often pinch far harder than the arm can hold up, or the reverse.

General End-Effector Thrust (angled lever)

For a linkage-driven joint where the actuator doesn't push perpendicular to its lever arm: F = τ_required / (r · sinθ). Force rises sharply as θ approaches 0°/180° — a shallow attachment angle needs disproportionately more force for the same torque.

Power & Current Budget

Running power sums each joint's mechanical power (required torque × target angular speed) at an assumed 60% electrical-to-mechanical efficiency. This is a running estimate, not a worst-case stall/jam current — enter each motor's datasheet stall current below to see what your supply must survive if multiple joints jam simultaneously (e.g. a collision), which is the scenario that actually browns out supplies.

Model Assumptions & Known Limitations

  • Quasi-static, not full manipulator dynamics. Gravity torque is computed exactly for this arm's real 3D geometry (τⱼ = zⱼ·Σ mₖ(pₖ−oⱼ)×(0,0,−g), summed only over masses genuinely downstream of joint j — see tab 08 for the corrected DH chain this is built on). The dynamic (inertial) term is a simplified point-mass + link self-inertia (mL²/12, parallel-axis) approximation. Coriolis and centripetal coupling between joints moving simultaneously are not modelled — those only matter for fast coordinated moves, and would add to (not subtract from) the numbers here.
  • Link self-inertia (mL²/12) assumes a roughly uniform mass distribution along the link's actual 3D length. If your COM fraction (c2/c3/c4) is far from 0.5 because of a concentrated motor mass, the true rotational inertia differs from this approximation — direction of the error isn't fixed, treat it as a secondary-order term.
  • Reflected motor/gearbox rotor inertia is included only if you fill in the optional rotor-inertia field per joint in Torque & Motors; left at zero, high-ratio drivetrains (>20:1) will under-report their true dynamic torque need.
  • Worst-case search is a discretised grid (20 steps per axis) over your θ2/θ3/θ4 joint-limit ranges, not a closed-form optimum — θ1 (base yaw) and θ5 (wrist roll) are held fixed during the search since neither changes any joint's gravity-torque magnitude (rotating the whole arm about its own vertical base axis doesn't change any link's height, and rolling the gripper about its own axis doesn't move anything off that axis). Resolution error is well under 1° — negligible next to your safety factor — but it is an approximation, not an exact solve.
  • Structural sizing is simple cantilever beam theory (bending stress + tip deflection on a hollow round tube), not FEA. Link lengths are the actual 3D distances between consecutive joint origins from the corrected DH chain (pose-independent by construction — rotating a joint doesn't stretch the rigid body after it). Stress concentrations at mounting holes, bearing seats and brackets aren't modelled and will see higher local stress than predicted here — treat the recommended tube as a lower bound, not a final part spec.
  • Continuous-duty derating is a rule of thumb (default 35% of stall), not your specific motor's thermal curve. If your motor's datasheet states an actual rated/continuous torque, use that number directly (enter it as "stall" and set derating to 100%) instead of relying on the generic ratio.
  • RC servo torque is voltage-dependent. A "20 kg·cm" servo is almost always rated at 7.4–8.4V — run it from 5V logic or a sagging battery and you can lose 30–40% of that figure. This tool has no way to know your actual supply voltage under load; budget margin for it.
  • Backdrivability / power-off holding is a mechanical property, not a torque number. A motor sized for exactly the required torque will NOT hold that load with power removed unless the drivetrain is self-locking (worm gear, very high ratio + low efficiency) or a mechanical brake is fitted — flagged per joint on the Torque & Motors tab.
  • Actuator placement is one of the biggest levers you have. Every gram of motor sitting further from the base multiplies through leverage at every joint above it. Your CAD's belt-driven elbow motor (mounted near the shoulder rather than at the elbow itself) is exactly this technique — set the elbow motor's mount dropdown in Setup to "Remote (at shoulder)" to see how much torque that removes from the shoulder joint.
  • Inverse kinematics is numeric (damped least-squares on the Jacobian), not closed-form — this arm's real geometry (180° elbow twist, offset base-to-shoulder rise) doesn't reduce to a clean law-of-cosines solve the way a pure planar 3-link arm does. It converges from your current Live Pose angles as the seed; different seeds can land on different elbow configurations. Backlash and structural compliance aren't modelled — both FK and IK assume rigid links and zero gear play.
  • This is a first-pass sizing tool, not a certified structural or safety analysis. Validate against real hardware before anything load-bearing or safety-critical.
  • Gravity g = 9.81 m/s²; all masses convert g→kg and all lengths mm→m internally before any calculation.
